Facebook groups for Nepalis in Australia are full of posts like "Best rate to Nepal, inbox me" or "Same-day transfer, cheaper than the bank". Some come from real, licensed businesses. Others are unregistered operators or outright scammers. This guide shows you how to tell the difference before you hand over any money.

First rule: a licensed provider must be on the AUSTRAC register

In Australia, any business that sends money overseas for customers must be registered with AUSTRAC, the government's financial crime regulator. AUSTRAC keeps a public list called the Remittance Sector Register. Anyone can search it for free.

  1. Ask the person or page for the business's legal name and ABN. A genuine agent will tell you straight away.
  2. Open the AUSTRAC register page and search by name (legal or trading name) or by ABN or ACN, typed without spaces.
  3. Check that the name, ABN and address match what you were told. A yellow warning symbol next to a result means AUSTRAC has put conditions on that business.
  4. Also check AUSTRAC's lists of suspended, cancelled and refused registrations on the same page.

AUSTRAC notes there can be a short delay before new details appear, so if a well-known brand is missing, contact the brand through its official website rather than through the person who messaged you.

Red flags on Facebook and messaging apps

  • "Inbox me for the rate." Licensed providers show their rate and fee on their website or app before you pay.
  • Pay into a personal bank account, or split the money across several accounts "to avoid limits".
  • No receipt and no tracking number, just "trust me, it will arrive".
  • A rate far better than every licensed provider. Compare it on the comparison page. If it looks too good to be true, it usually is.
  • Pressure to act now: "rate only valid for 10 minutes", "last slot today".
  • Cash handed over in a car park or at a house, with no shopfront or registered business.
  • They refuse to give an ABN or legal business name, or the name doesn't match the AUSTRAC register.
  • They ask for your online banking password or a one-time code. No real provider ever needs these.

Many of these "agents" are running hundi. Even when the money arrives, it is illegal in Nepal and Australia and you have no protection if it doesn't. Read why you should never use hundi.

Fake pages that copy real brands

Scammers also copy the logo and name of well-known services. Before you pay:

  • Type the provider's web address yourself, or use their official app from the App Store or Google Play. Don't follow a link someone sent you.
  • Check the page's age and history. A brand page created last month with few posts is a warning sign.
  • Remember that real support staff won't contact you first by personal message asking you to "re-send" a transfer.

AUSTRAC itself also warns that scammers pretend to be AUSTRAC staff. It says it will never demand payment or your personal details by email or phone. See AUSTRAC's frauds and scams page.

Check the Nepal side too

In Nepal, money must be paid out by a bank, wallet or remittance company licensed by Nepal Rastra Bank. Tell your family to collect only from a real branch or agent of the named company, or into their own bank account or wallet, and never to share the tracking number (PIN or MTCN) with anyone who calls them.

If you think you've been scammed

AUSTRAC's advice, in order, is:

  1. Contact your bank immediately. The sooner they know, the better the chance of stopping or recovering a payment.
  2. Get support from IDCARE, Australia's identity and cyber support service, especially if you shared ID documents.
  3. Report it to Scamwatch at scamwatch.gov.au/report-a-scam, or to your local police. Online scams can also be reported to the Australian Cyber Security Centre.

Then warn others in the Facebook group, without sharing your own personal details. Reporting helps stop the same person targeting the next family.

The safe way

Use a provider you can find on the AUSTRAC register, pay through its own website or app, and keep the receipt. Licensed services compete hard on the Australia to Nepal route, so the safe option is often just as cheap. Compare licensed providers or follow the step-by-step guide to sending money to Nepal.
